What is a Hanok?

Architecture that listens to weather.

For a thousand years, Koreans built houses that worked with the climate — not against it. Ondol warmed the floor in winter. Maru, a raised wooden hall, cooled the body in summer. Hanji paper, glued to the wooden lattice of windows, let air pass but held heat. The house didn’t fight the seasons. It listened.

A hanok stay is not a museum night. It is a way of sleeping that the modern world has mostly forgotten.

온돌 · Ondol

Floor heating since the Bronze Age. Flue gases from the kitchen warm flat stones beneath the floor. You don’t feel heat rise — you feel the earth warm under you.

마루 · Maru

An elevated wooden hall, open to the courtyard. The summer’s answer to ondol. Take a nap here at three in the afternoon.

한지 · Hanji

Mulberry paper windows. They glow at dawn before sunrise reaches you. They breathe — humidity drops 30% on rainy days.

마당 · Madang

The inner courtyard. Half-sky, half-ground. In old Korea, weddings, funerals, and harvest all happened in the madang.

Architecture

Read a hanok by its shape.

Hanok plans follow Korean letters — ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㅁ, 一 — depending on climate, family size, and the slope of the land. Choose by what the shape gives you.

一

Il-jaja (一字)

Straight bar

Single row of rooms. South-facing. Common in warm southern Korea. Best for: solo, couples, quiet writing retreats.

ㄱ

Giyeok-jip (ㄱ字)

L-shape

An L wraps a small courtyard. Two wings: one to live in, one to rest in. Best for: small families, contemplative stays.

ㄷ

Digeut-jip (ㄷ字)

U-shape

Three sides around a courtyard. Generous, ceremonial. Best for: family reunions, intergenerational stays.

ㅁ

Mieum-jip (ㅁ字)

Closed square

Four sides enclose an inner sky. Traditional in cold northern Korea. Best for: privacy, winter stays, full house bookings.

Regions

Eight Koreas to sleep in.

Hanok is not one thing. A Jeju hanok is built of stone against the wind. A Bukchon hanok stands shoulder-to-shoulder with the king’s palace. Choose the Korea you want.

Bukchon · 북촌

Seoul · Between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung Palaces

The royal scholars’ quarter. 900 hanoks survive on stone-walled lanes. Five minutes to two UNESCO palaces. Mornings here belong to slow walkers.

28 hosts

Seochon · 서촌

Seoul · West of Gyeongbokgung Palace

The poets’ and painters’ village. Quieter than Bukchon, lived-in by locals, bookshops and cafés in 1920s hanok shells.

14 hosts

Ikseon-dong · 익선동

Seoul · Jongno

Korea’s first planned hanok neighborhood (1920s), now reborn as a hanok-hipster alley. Stay above a coffee roaster, below a vintage shop.

9 hosts

Jeonju · 전주 한옥마을

North Jeolla · 2hr from Seoul by KTX

The largest hanok village in Korea — 800+ traditional houses, all lived-in. The bibimbap capital. Wear hanbok and walk to Gyeonggijeon Shrine.

31 hosts

Gyeongju · 경주

North Gyeongsang · The thousand-year capital of Silla

Sleep beside royal burial mounds. UNESCO Yangdong Folk Village — a hanok village preserved exactly as it stood in the 1400s.

12 hosts

Andong · 안동 하회마을

North Gyeongsang · UNESCO World Heritage

Hahoe Village — a hanok village inside a bend in the Nakdong River. Confucian scholars’ houses, mask dance, soju distilleries.

Jeju · 제주

Volcanic Island, South Sea

Jeju hanok is different — black volcanic stone walls, thatched roof tied against the wind, a small low doorway. The island’s own architecture.

Gangneung · 강릉

East Coast · Mountains meet the sea

The home of Ojukheon, a 500-year-old hanok where Korea’s greatest Confucian scholar was born. Pine forests, anchovy-broth breakfasts.
