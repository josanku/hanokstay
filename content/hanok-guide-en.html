1. What is a hanok?

A hanok (한옥, 韓屋) is a Korean traditional house — but that description undersells it. A hanok is a system of ideas about how to live with the Korean climate, the Korean landscape, and the Korean family.

Three things make a building a hanok:

  • A timber post-and-beam frame, joined without nails using interlocking wood.
  • A floor that can be heated from below (ondol) and a raised wooden hall (maru) for summer.
  • Doors and windows of mulberry paper (hanji) glued to wooden lattices.

Hanoks were built across Korea for at least 700 years — from royal palaces to farmers’ cottages. The same logic scaled up and down.

2. The five elements

온돌 · Ondol — heat from below

Ondol is Korea’s underfloor heating system, in use since around the Bronze Age and refined by the Goryeo dynasty. A fire in the kitchen sends hot gases through stone flues laid horizontally beneath the floor. The stones (gudeul) absorb the heat and release it slowly through the night.

The Korean body learned to sleep on a warm floor, not in a high bed. This is why you sleep on a thin mattress directly on the floor in a hanok — the floor itself is the heat source.

“A Korean child knows the warmest corner of every room. It is the corner closest to the firebox.”

마루 · Maru — the summer hall

If ondol is for winter, maru is for summer. Maru is a raised wooden floor, open on at least one side to the courtyard, slightly elevated above the ground for ventilation. On a hot August afternoon, the maru is fifteen degrees cooler than the courtyard. You lie on it. You doze.

Every hanok of any size has both an ondol room and a maru hall. The Korean house contains two climates.

한지 · Hanji — paper windows

Hanji is paper made from mulberry bark, glued to a wooden lattice to form doors and windows. It is translucent, not transparent. Morning light arrives diffused, soft, as if the room were inside a paper lantern.

Hanji also breathes. It releases humidity in summer and holds it in winter. Conservation scientists have measured 30% humidity stabilization in hanji-windowed rooms vs glass-windowed rooms. A hanji window is a climate device.

You will hear hanji. When wind passes, the paper sighs.

마당 · Madang — the courtyard

The madang is the open ground at the center of a hanok compound. It is not a garden — it is bare, swept, packed earth. Korean tradition resists planting in the madang, because the madang is a stage. Weddings happen here. Funerals. Rice spread to dry. Children playing. The madang is the part of the house open to the sky.

기와 · Giwa — the tiled roof

A hanok with a clay-tile roof is called giwajip (기와집); with a thatched roof, chogajip (초가집). Giwa is dark grey, made from local clay, fired and curved. The roof line of a hanok curves upward at the eaves — visually light, structurally to shed snow and rain. The curve is unmistakably Korean: less dramatic than Chinese roofs, more lifted than Japanese.

3. Why hanoks are shaped like Korean letters

Look at a hanok from above and it reads as a Korean letter — ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㅁ, 一. The shape isn’t aesthetic; it’s climatic.

  • 一 (Il-jaja) — a single straight bar. Common in southern Korea, where summer heat needs cross-ventilation. South-facing for winter sun.
  • ㄱ (Giyeok-jip) — an L. Two wings meet at a corner, sheltering a partial courtyard from the north wind.
  • ㄴ (Nieun-jip) — a mirrored L, less common.
  • ㄷ (Digeut-jip) — a U. Three sides around a courtyard. Generous for extended families.
  • ㅁ (Mieum-jip) — a closed square enclosing an inner sky-courtyard. Traditional in cold northern Korea where wind blocking matters most.

The same family lived in different shapes in different regions. The shape is the climate’s drawing.

4. Regional differences

There is no one hanok. The hanoks of Seoul are not the hanoks of Jeju. A short tour:

Bukchon (Seoul)

The royal-scholar quarter, founded in the 14th century and rebuilt 1920s–30s as the first “modern” hanok district. Bukchon hanoks are smaller, tighter, with steeper roofs to fit narrow city plots. The famous tile-roof skyline rises between two royal palaces.

Jeonju Hanok Village

The largest hanok cluster in Korea — over 800 hanoks, almost all still residences, restaurants, or stays. Jeonju is also the birthplace of bibimbap. The hanok village is a 5-minute walk from the Joseon Dynasty’s ancestral shrine.

Gyeongju & Andong Hahoe

Two UNESCO World Heritage hanok villages. Hahoe (Andong) sits inside a bend of the Nakdong River — Confucian scholar-aristocrat houses, the famous Hahoe mask dance, soju distilleries. Gyeongju’s Yangdong Village is a 500-year-old aristocratic clan village.

Jeju Island

Jeju hanok is its own architecture. Walls are black volcanic basalt, dry-stacked. Roofs are thatched and roped against the wind in a grid pattern that holds against typhoons. Doorways are low — the wind passes over you. Inside, the smoke-blackened ceiling.

5. A short history

Pre-history to Goryeo (—1392): Ondol systems date to at least the Iron Age. By the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), the basic hanok plan is fixed.

Joseon dynasty (1392–1897): Hanok flourishes. Strict Confucian rules govern who builds what — aristocrats can build with tiled roofs and elaborate brackets; commoners cannot. Bukchon takes shape as the aristocratic quarter.

Colonial period (1910–1945): Korea is occupied by Japan. Bukchon and other Seoul hanok districts are rebuilt 1920s–30s as the first “urban hanok” — denser, smaller, with modern materials like glass mixed in. This is the hanok most tourists see today in Seoul.

Post-war (1953–1990s): Hanok is seen as backward. Apartments are the future. 95% of urban hanoks are demolished.

2000s onwards: A reversal. Hanok preservation laws. Tax breaks for hanok owners. Hanok cafés. Hanok stays. Today there are roughly 9,000 registered hanok stays and traditional hanoks across Korea, with hanok experience businesses licensed in every major hanok cluster.

6. What it’s like to stay in one

You arrive in the late afternoon. The host opens a wooden gate. You take your shoes off — always — at the threshold where the stone steps meet the wooden floor.

Inside is smaller than you expect. The room is square, mostly empty. There is no bed. There is a folded bedding (yo mattress and ibul blanket) in a closet, which you will unfold on the floor at night and refold in the morning. The floor is warm under your socks. The hanji windows glow.

Dinner, if the host serves it, is small. Many small dishes. Rice. Soup. Pickled things. You sit on the floor at a low table.

At night you lie on the warm floor. The hanji blocks streetlights into a soft amber. If wind passes, the paper sighs. You sleep harder than you expected.

At dawn you wake before alarms. Hanji lets in light differently. The host has set a kettle on. There is tea.

7. Etiquette

  • Shoes off at the threshold. Always. Step from the stone-step onto the wood floor — no shoes cross that line.
  • Don’t sit on the threshold itself. In Korean tradition, the threshold is the spirit of the house. Step over it, not on it.
  • Use the bedding closet. Bedding is for unfolding at night. In the morning, fold and put away.
  • Speak softly in the courtyard. Sound carries through hanji.
  • Don’t prop hanji doors open with force. The lattice is delicate. The host will show you how.
  • If you bathe in a traditional bath, rinse first. The bath is for soaking after washing, not washing.
  • Receive things with two hands from your host — tea, food, gifts. It’s a small courtesy and they will notice.

8. The legal status of hanok stays

This matters more than tourists realize. In Korea, short-term stays in non-licensed homes are technically illegal — this is why much of Airbnb’s Korea inventory exists in grey zones. Hanokstay by Wehome lists only legally registered operators under one of these licenses:

  • 한옥체험업 (Hanok Experience Business) — the primary license for hanok stays, under the Tourism Promotion Act (관광진흥법). Requires the building to be a registered hanok and the host to be licensed by the local government.
  • 외국인관광 도시민박업 (Foreign Tourist Urban B&B) — licenses urban homestays where the host lives on-site and rents rooms to foreign tourists.
  • 농어촌민박업 (Rural Tourism B&B) — for hanoks in rural villages.

Every listing on Hanokstay shows its license type and registration number. We verify it before listing.

9. Hanok vs Japanese ryokan vs Chinese siheyuan

Travelers often ask. Briefly:

  • Ryokan (Japan) shares the floor-sleeping (futon) and paper-screen (shoji) elements, but the Japanese floor is unheated tatami mat. There is no ondol equivalent. Ryokans are often inns with multiple rooms; hanoks are often one family’s house.
  • Siheyuan (China) shares the courtyard-house logic with hanok’s ㅁ-shape, but Chinese houses use kang beds (heated platforms, not heated floors), tiled floors, and a different roof curve.
  • Hanok is the only East Asian house tradition built around floor heating as the primary winter strategy. It is why Koreans sit and sleep on the floor — the floor is the warmest place in the room.

10. How to choose your hanok

A short field guide for first-time hanok stays:

  • If you have one night in Seoul: Bukchon or Seochon. Walk to two palaces in the morning.
  • If you want the village feel: Jeonju Hanok Village. Largest and most lived-in.
  • If you care about old: Andong Hahoe or Gyeongju Yangdong. Both UNESCO. Hanoks here are 300–500 years old.
  • If you want quiet: Gangneung or a rural hanok in North Gyeongsang. Smaller villages, fewer tourists.
  • If you want strange: Jeju. Black stone hanok is a different architecture altogether.
  • If you want the host: Filter by “host on-site.” In Korea, the best hanok stays are with a hanok keeper who tells you the house’s story.
