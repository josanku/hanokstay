Architecture

Hanok Architecture, Explained

From the timber frame to the curved eave — a structural tour of the Korean traditional house, written for travelers who want to understand what they’re sleeping in.

The frame: post-and-beam, no nails

A hanok stands on wood. Pine columns rise from carved stone bases (the stones lift the wood off wet earth so it doesn’t rot). Horizontal beams interlock with the columns using jointing — wooden tabs cut into the wood, slotted together. No nails. No glue. If you disassemble a hanok, you get a pile of numbered pieces that can be reassembled elsewhere.

The reason is earthquakes and humidity. A nailed joint cracks; a wooden interlock flexes. A hanok dating to 1500 is still standing because every piece was meant to be replaced one at a time.

The roof: the curve that names the country

Look at the roofline of a hanok and you see a curve — concave, lifted slightly at the corners. This is the cheoma (처마), the deep eave. Three things to know:

  • The cheoma is sun-tuned. It overhangs just far enough to block summer’s high sun from entering the room — and just little enough to let winter’s low sun in. The angle was calculated for Korean latitude.
  • The curve is structural. A flat eave sags. A curved eave distributes the weight of the tiles to the corner columns.
  • The lift is aesthetic. Korean tradition reads heaviness as ungainly. The lifted corner suggests the roof is rising, not falling.

The bracket system: dapo, jusimpo

Walk into a temple or a royal palace and you see complicated wooden brackets stacked beneath the eaves. These are gongpo (공포) — bracket sets that transfer the roof’s weight onto the columns. There are two grammars:

  • Jusimpo (주심포) — brackets only on top of the columns. Simpler, older, more austere. Confucian scholar houses.
  • Dapo (다포) — brackets on top of columns AND between them. Ornate. Reserved for royal palaces, temples, and the very highest aristocratic homes.

The hanok you stay in will almost certainly be jusimpo or simpler. Dapo is palace-grade.

Walls: not load-bearing

This is the surprise. In a hanok, walls don’t carry the building — the columns and beams do. Walls are infill: timber lattice packed with earth, straw, and lime, then plastered. Or they are sliding doors of hanji. Or they are entirely removable wooden panels.

Practically: in summer you can take down the entire south wall of a hanok by sliding it up and hooking it to the eave. The room becomes a porch. The maru becomes the room.

The plan: rooms named for function

안채 · Anchae
The inner quarters, traditionally for women and family. Faces the inner courtyard. Contains the kitchen and main living rooms.
사랑채 · Sarangchae
The outer quarters, for the male head of household and male guests. Often more refined finishes — this is where guests are received and books are kept.
행랑채 · Haengnangchae
The servants’ quarters, near the front gate. In modern hanok stays this is often a guest annex.
사당 · Sadang
The ancestral shrine. A small enclosed room or pavilion, usually at the back of the compound, where ancestral tablets were kept.
대청 · Daecheong
The main hall — a wide wooden floor between the rooms, used for ceremonies and meals. The architectural heart of the house.
부엌 · Bueok
The kitchen. Crucial because it’s the source of the ondol heat. Kitchens were sunken below floor level so the fire could feed under the adjacent rooms.
장독대 · Jangdokdae
The platform of fermentation pots — kimchi, doenjang (soybean paste), ganjang (soy sauce). Usually in the courtyard or against a sunny back wall.
마당 · Madang
The inner courtyard, the stage of the house. Kept bare and swept.

The four directions: facing south is not optional

Korean tradition orients the main rooms south, the kitchen east or west, the back wall north. This is climate-driven: south gets the most winter sun, north blocks winter wind, east catches morning light for the kitchen. A hanok facing any direction other than south is doing something unusual — often because of mountains or rivers that override the rule.

You will sense it when you stay. South-facing hanok rooms feel different at 3pm in January.

The materials: every part is Korea

  • Pine from Korean mountains (red pine, sonamu) — for columns and beams.
  • Clay from local earth — for walls, tiles, and the ondol flue stones.
  • Mulberry bark from local trees — pulped, screened, dried into hanji paper.
  • Granite — the foundation stones, often left rough.
  • Straw — for thatched commoner roofs, replaced annually.

A hanok is, in this sense, a Korean inventory. You can’t build one elsewhere from local materials and have it still be a hanok. The building is the country.
